The basics

Do cover letters still matter?

Short answer: when they're specific, yes. A generic letter adds nothing — but a tailored one does three things a resume can't:

Shows real interest

Anyone can mass-apply. A letter that names the company's product, mission, or recent work proves you actually want this job.

Connects the dots

Career changers, fresh graduates, and anyone with an unusual path can explain the "why" behind their application in two sentences.

Gives you a voice

Resumes are bullet points. A letter lets tone, enthusiasm, and judgment show — the things interviews are actually about.

Rule of thumb: write one whenever the posting asks for it, and whenever you have something specific to say about the company. Skip the generic version — it signals the opposite of caring.

The structure

The 4 paragraphs that work

Hiring managers skim. Give each paragraph exactly one job:

1. The hook (2–3 lines)

Role + why this company, specifically. "I'm applying for the Junior ML Engineer role because your work on Urdu-language NLP is exactly the problem I spent my final year on." Never open with "I am writing to apply for…"

2. The proof (4–6 lines)

Your two strongest, most relevant achievements — with numbers. This is the resume's greatest hits, not its rerun: pick results that map to the posting's requirements.

3. The fit (2–3 lines)

Connect your proof to their needs. "Your posting asks for PyTorch and deployment experience — I shipped a PyTorch classifier to production as…" shows you read the ad.

4. The close (2 lines)

Enthusiasm + easy next step. "I'd love to discuss how I can contribute to [team]. Thank you for your time — I'm available at [phone] this week." Short, confident, done.

Annotated example

A full example, line by line

Fictional example — replace every bracket with your own details.

Dear [Hiring Manager's Name],

I'm applying for the [exact job title] role because [one specific thing about the company — a product, a launch, a value — that genuinely interests you]. ← Hook: role + specific reason, not "I am writing to apply".

In my [current/most recent role or project], I [strongest achievement with a number] and [second achievement with a number]. ← Proof: two quantified wins that match the posting.

Your posting emphasizes [requirement 1] and [requirement 2] — both are where I've done my best work: [one-line connection]. ← Fit: mirror their language back with evidence.

I'd welcome the chance to discuss how I can contribute to [team/company]. Thank you for your consideration — I can be reached at [phone] or [email]. ← Close: confident, easy next step.

Sincerely,
[Your Name]

Notice what's missing: no life story, no "I believe I would be a great fit" without proof, no salary talk, no repeating the resume bullet-for-bullet.

Mistakes to avoid

7 ways letters die

1. Repeating the resume

If a sentence just restates a bullet point, delete it. The letter must say something new: why this company, why now.

2. "To whom it may concern"

Find a name if you can; otherwise "Dear Hiring Manager". The 1998 salutation reads as a mass mail-merge.

3. All about me, nothing about them

"I want this job because it will help my career" is honest and fatal. Flip it: what do they get?

4. Vague enthusiasm

"I'm passionate about excellence" means nothing. "I rebuilt our onboarding flow and cut drop-off 30%" means everything.

5. Salary expectations, unasked

Only include them if the posting explicitly asks. Otherwise you anchor the negotiation early for zero benefit.

6. Typos and wrong names

One wrong company name ("Dear Google" sent to Meta) ends the application. Read it aloud once before sending.

7. Two pages

If it doesn't fit one page, it's a memoir. Cut ruthlessly — every sentence must earn its place.

ATS note: many systems parse cover letters too, so keep the same formatting rules as your resume — real text, no graphics — and weave in a few of the job ad's key terms naturally.

FAQ

Frequently asked questions

Do I still need a cover letter in 2026?

When it's optional, a good one still helps — it shows genuine interest and connects your experience to the role in a way a resume can't. When it's required, skipping it is an instant filter. Write one whenever the posting asks, and whenever you have something specific to say about why this company and this role.

How long should a cover letter be?

Under 300 words — three to four short paragraphs on one page. Hiring managers skim; every sentence must earn its place. If yours needs a second page, cut it in half.

Should I use the same cover letter for every job?

No. A generic letter is worse than none — it signals you didn't care enough to tailor it. Keep a reusable structure, but rewrite the hook and proof paragraphs for every application, referencing the company and the specific role.

Who do I address the cover letter to?

A named hiring manager if you can find one (check the posting or the company's site). Otherwise "Dear Hiring Manager" is fine. Avoid "To whom it may concern".

Do cover letters go through the ATS too?

Often, yes — many systems parse cover letters alongside resumes, so the same formatting rules apply: real text, no graphics, and a few of the job ad's key terms woven in naturally.

What's the biggest cover letter mistake?

Repeating your resume in paragraph form. The letter's job is to answer one question the resume can't: why this role, at this company, right now. If a sentence just restates a bullet point, delete it and say something new.

Should I mention salary expectations in a cover letter?

Only if the posting explicitly asks for them. Otherwise it anchors the negotiation early and for no benefit. Keep the letter about fit and enthusiasm.

Can AI write my cover letter for me?

AI can draft the structure, but generic AI letters are easy to spot — vague praise, no specifics, no voice. Use a tool for the skeleton, then rewrite it with real details about the company and your own measurable results. That specificity is what gets interviews.
